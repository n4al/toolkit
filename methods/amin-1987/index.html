Amin (1987)

A Note on the Concept of Delinking

Samir Amin. Review (Fernand Braudel Center), 10(3), 435–444.

Find the published article →

Methodological profile

Amin develops the concept of delinking through an illustrative comparison of two development strategies. The article combines theoretical argument with simplified comparative modelling. It does not provide an empirical estimate of unequal-exchange flows.

1. Theoretical mechanism

Amin defines delinking as a development strategy in which economic choices are organised according to nationally grounded and popular priorities rather than subordinated to the world-capitalist law of value.

Delinking does not mean eliminating international economic relations. It means making those relations subordinate to the requirements of internally oriented development.

2. Research and estimation strategy

Amin compares two strategies for an initially identical, predominantly rural economy over a ten-year period.

The first follows a national-popular, autocentric development strategy. The second follows the world-capitalist law of value and externally oriented accumulation.

The comparison holds several assumptions constant, including population growth, productivity improvements and investment requirements, in order to illustrate how different economic priorities affect development and distribution.

3. Analytical and computational techniques

The article uses a simplified comparative model involving rural and urban production, relative prices, labour allocation, productivity, investment and income distribution.

Amin presents an initial comparison in Table 1 and describes the outcomes of the two strategies after ten years.

The article does not provide the full technical derivation. Amin refers readers to The Future of Maoism (1982), pp. 7–37, for the more detailed argument.

4. Findings and measured quantities

The illustrative models generate different outcomes for income distribution and development priorities.

Amin reports annual national-income growth of 4.9% in the national-popular model and assumes the same GDP growth rate in the world-capitalist model. The comparison therefore draws attention to differences in distribution and development structure, rather than simply comparing aggregate growth rates.

These are illustrative model outcomes, not independently verified historical estimates or measurements of unequal exchange.

Assumptions and limitations

The comparison begins with a stylised economy and relies on specified assumptions about population, productivity, investment and distribution.

Amin explicitly acknowledges that the models do not establish why a particular strategy is chosen or identify the social and ideological determinants of that choice.

The Toolkit has not independently reproduced the calculations. The detailed technical argument in Amin (1982) remains to be examined.

Relevance to the Toolkit

This publication provides an example of comparative modelling used to investigate alternative economic relationships and development priorities.

It connects the study of dependency and unequal development to questions about how economic relationships might be reorganised. It should not be treated as an unequal-exchange measurement method.

Related research instrument

The Model & Capacity Audit can be used to examine the capacities assumed, exercised or produced in Amin's illustrative development strategies.

It provides a structured way to investigate questions that the model leaves open, including how the political, organisational, productive and institutional capacities required to establish and sustain different development priorities might be formed and reproduced.

The audit does not reproduce Amin's calculations, validate the model or establish the feasibility of delinking. It helps identify assumptions, formulate further research questions and specify the evidence required to investigate them.
