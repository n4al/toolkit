METHODS LIBRARY / RESEARCH PROFILE 001

Dorninger et al. (2021): Global patterns of ecologically unequal exchange

A documented account of how one study traces embodied resources, trade in value added and statistical relationships across the world economy.

Corrected R model executed — close fit agreement; exact replication unresolved

Primary source. Dorninger, C., Hornborg, A., Abson, D. J., et al. (2021). “Global patterns of ecologically unequal exchange: Implications for sustainability in the 21st century.” Ecological Economics 179, 106824. DOI / publisher page . This profile is an independent educational reconstruction, not author-endorsed code.

1. Research question and scope

The authors examine international asymmetries in embodied materials, energy, land and labour, alongside monetary value added, over 1990–2015. Their global database covers 189 countries, while their reported analytical sample covers 170 countries. For comparisons they classify countries into income-based groups, treating China and India separately. These categories are specific to this study.

2. Keep the analytical levels distinct

Level What the paper uses What it establishes
Theoretical approach Ecologically unequal exchange A framework for investigating asymmetric resource transfers and monetary compensation.
Accounting technique Environmentally extended multi-regional input–output analysis (EEMRIO), using Eora v199.82 Traces direct and upstream inputs embodied in final demand and trade.
Measured quantities Raw material equivalents, energy, land and labour Physical resource requirements in different units; embodied labour is an indicator, not a stand-alone computational technique.
Monetary counterpart Trade in Value Added (TiVA) Locates monetary value added embodied in supply chains and compares it with exported resource units.
Statistical technique Piecewise structural equation modelling (SEM) Tests specified statistical relationships; model fit alone does not establish causal identification.

3. Core accounting logic

Input–output transactions yield a direct-input coefficient matrix A and Leontief inverse L = (I − A)⁻¹. With final demand y, physical extensions e, output x and value added v, the paper defines physical intensity q = e / x and value-added intensity p = v / x. The corresponding footprint matrices are F = q̂Lŷ and B = p̂Lŷ, where hats indicate diagonal matrices. These accounting matrices allocate upstream physical inputs and value added to final demand. See the published equations in sections 3.1–3.2; this is a conceptual transcription, not executable Eora code.

4. Statistical model: materials

Figure 4a depicts five component regressions involving reserves, income, technological adoption, military expenditure, net imports of raw material equivalents and value added per exported material unit. The paper specifies linear models for technology and net imports, and Gamma models with log links for income, military expenditure and value-added outcomes. The authors used R and piecewiseSEM. Their reported overall materials-model fit is Fisher’s C = 2.57, p = 0.63.

6. Independent reconstruction

The accompanying R script is an independently authored attempt to reconstruct the five materials regressions and a piecewiseSEM model from the publisher’s 2015 supplementary spreadsheet. Download the spreadsheet from the publisher page and follow the script’s instructions. The initial script (v1) was executed in R on 17 September 2026, revealing a model-specification error: it included a technology → military path absent from the published 13-path diagram. The linked corrected v2 script was subsequently executed in Posit Cloud, and its global goodness-of-fit statistics closely match the published figure. Neither version is author-supplied code or a verified exact replication.

Coefficient audit — v1 only. Several unstandardized coefficients from the original complete-case R run closely matched transcribed Figure 4a values: reserves → net materials −8.1202 versus −8.120; income → net materials 0.4161 versus 0.416; and military → net materials −11.5381 versus −11.542. These are v1 results; they must not be attributed to the corrected v2 model without its coefficient output.

Remaining limits. The supplementary worksheet contains 170 country rows; v1's common complete-case sample was 130, and the corrected script applies the same sample restriction, although the v2 sample count was not separately supplied in the final screenshot. The authors' precise missing-data and modelling settings are not fully established. The full 1990–2015 EEMRIO calculations have not been reproduced. The audit therefore supports a successful independent reconstruction with close global-fit agreement, not an exact or full-paper replication.

7. Interpretation and limitations

The study distinguishes asymmetric physical transfers from a claim that commodities possess an objectively measurable value above their market price. Its country-level design cannot resolve within-country appropriation or historical emergence; the authors also identify limits in their technological-infrastructure proxy and sensitivity of EEMRIO estimates to database construction and environmental extensions. Statistical associations should not be read as proof of a single causal mechanism.

8. Connection to the Relinking Lab

The paper investigates how resource transfers and value-added patterns relate to the distribution of productive infrastructure. The Relinking Lab asks a distinct, additional question: how might particular productive, technical and organisational capacities be assembled and reproduced through changing relationships? This is a proposed research extension, not a finding derived from the paper’s model.

9. What this method establishes — and what it does not

Dorninger et al. combine environmentally extended multiregional input–output analysis, value-added measures and structural equation modelling. These components answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable evidence.

What the method can investigate

  • Embodied resource flows: the distribution of materials, energy, land and labour embodied in international production and consumption.
  • Physical and monetary asymmetries: relationships between countries' embodied-resource trade positions and monetary returns.
  • Statistical associations: relationships among the variables included in the specified structural equation model.

Limits of inference

Net physical transfers do not automatically establish monetary value transfers. Differences in monetary returns do not, by themselves, identify a unique causal mechanism. A satisfactory structural equation model fit does not establish that its specified relationships are causal.

The 2015 country-level model also cannot, on its own, establish historical trajectories or identify how benefits and costs are distributed among actors within countries.

Independent reconstruction

The Toolkit independently reconstructed the 2015 materials structural equation model using the publisher's supplementary data. After correcting an extra relationship in the initial implementation, the R model produced Fisher's C = 2.531 (p = 0.639), closely matching the published Fisher's C = 2.57 (p = 0.63).

This is close agreement in global model fit, not verification of every coefficient or of the upstream input–output calculations. Exact replication remains unresolved.

Questions for further research

Further work could examine sensitivity to the underlying input–output database, differences in sectoral composition, competing explanations for monetary asymmetries, changes over time and distributional outcomes within countries. Investigating how these relationships might be transformed requires additional evidence about productive, institutional and political capacities.
